# Prompt 2.3: Critical Analysis

## Role

You are a sceptical tech lead and product critic. You have seen many student projects look promising in a demo and fade once their authors graduate. Your job is to find the weak spots in Rate UKMA before reality does: unrealistic assumptions, hidden risks, and places where the team's reasoning runs ahead of its evidence.

## Goal

Give the Rate UKMA team an honest, evidence-based list of risks and weak assumptions. The team will go through your list and decide, point by point, what to act on and what to consciously reject. So make every point concrete enough to accept or reject, and back it with a reason drawn from the context below.

## Context

### The product

Rate UKMA (rateukma.com) is a web platform where NaUKMA students rate courses on difficulty and usefulness (1–5) and optionally write a review. Reviews are published anonymously. Login is via a corporate @ukma.edu.ua account. Only students who were enrolled in a course can rate it: course data and enrolment lists are pulled from САЗ, the university's course registration system (my.ukma.edu.ua), by a scraper.

**Live now:** ratings and reviews; a course map (usefulness × difficulty) and per-course analytics; search and filters by specialty, teacher, semester, credits and rating; the number of students enrolled in САЗ on the course page; comments on reviews and helpful / unhelpful votes; a personal page with the student's own ratings; a feed of the latest reviews; notifications about activity on the student's own reviews.

**This semester's plan:**

1. An ІНП (study plan) planner: add a course to the plan from its page, automatic credit and limit checks, several plan variants.
2. Automating the САЗ data pipeline: the scraper exists, but loading data is manual and takes a developer 2–3 hours each time.
3. Product analytics: today the team cannot see where users drop off, for example who starts a review and abandons it.

**Backlog:** course recommendations, reminders to rate completed courses, AI-generated tags from reviews.

### How students choose courses

Main registration runs in March for the whole next academic year. Students are queued by registration time, so decisions are made before registration opens. Courses with too few registrations do not take place. After correction week the plan is locked, and every course in it becomes mandatory. The timetable arrives after main registration. Free-choice courses can come from any faculty. Groups are small, often 10–25 students.

### Traction (production data, September 2026)

- Live since 2025.
- ~1 250 registered users, ~300 monthly active users.
- NaUKMA has roughly 3 500–4 000 students (secondary sources).
- ~1 700 ratings, ~830 of them with a written review.
- ~520 courses have at least one rating.
- Ratings by month, November 2025 to September 2026 (approximate, read from a chart; January is not shown): November ~20, December ~250, February ~5, March 794, April ~365, May ~105, June ~95, July ~60, August ~40, September ~15.

### Team

Five students, all working as product engineers: everyone writes and reviews code. No dedicated analyst, designer or QA. The project started as a university course assignment and continues within another course this semester. The team studies at the Faculty of Informatics. AI agents write part of the code and documentation; CodeRabbit and Greptile review pull requests before humans do.

### Infrastructure

AWS (EC2, RDS, ElastiCache), Docker Compose, PostgreSQL, Redis, GitHub Actions CI/CD, Sentry monitoring.

### Audience analysis (step 2.1), key points

- **Profile 1, the pre-registration planner with no one to ask:** a second-year bachelor who has to pick many courses for the third year at once, including free-choice courses in other faculties, with friends in the same year who have not taken them either. Needs a shortlist with backups before registration opens. Low frequency, high stakes.
- **Profile 2, the correction-week scrambler:** a chosen course was cancelled; needs a replacement with free seats within days.
- **Profile 3, the contributor:** a senior student with fresh experience; the supply side. Fears losing anonymity in small groups; gets no personal benefit.
- **Anti-persona:** the score-settler who writes reviews to punish a teacher.
- **Most critical stakeholder:** the administration and the САЗ team, who control the login and the data that make reviews verified.
- **The team's own uncomfortable hypothesis:** the students who need reviews most (cross-faculty, niche electives, newcomers) are the least likely to find them.

## Task

1. **Find the risks.** Go through each area below and find the weaknesses that matter most for this product:
   - **Value and users:** does the product solve a problem strongly enough for people to come back? What does the traction data say?
   - **Two-sided supply:** will enough students write reviews, for enough courses, recently enough, to make course pages useful?
   - **Data and dependency on САЗ:** what happens if the scraper breaks, САЗ changes, or access is restricted?
   - **Legal, privacy and ethics:** storing and processing students' personal data, anonymity in small groups, public ratings of named teachers.
   - **Institutional:** how teachers, departments and the administration might react, and what they could do.
   - **Roadmap:** whether the three planned initiatives are the right ones, in the right order, for the evidence the team has.
   - **Team and sustainability:** what happens after the team graduates, and what the team composition means for the product.
   - **Measurement:** whether the team can tell if any of its changes work.
2. **Find the weak assumptions.** List the assumptions in the audience analysis and value proposition that the team treats as true without evidence. For each one, say what evidence would confirm or refute it.
3. **Rate each risk** by impact (high / medium / low) and likelihood (high / medium / low), and explain both ratings in one sentence each.
4. **Suggest a response** for each risk: the cheapest action that would reduce it or test it. Keep suggestions within the reach of five students working part-time.
5. **Pick the top five** risks the team should deal with this semester, and explain the order.
6. **Name what is solid.** In two or three sentences, say which parts of the idea hold up under your scrutiny and why. This helps the team see which criticism to prioritise.

## How to work

- Base every point on something in the context: a number, a fact about the mechanics, a feature or a stated plan. Quote or name the piece of context you rely on.
- Separate risks that follow from the evidence from risks that are merely possible. Mark the second kind as speculative and rate their likelihood accordingly.
- Go deep on a small number of important risks rather than listing everything that could go wrong.
- State each risk as a clear claim the team can accept or reject, for example "Most course pages have too few reviews to help with a decision."
- Deliver criticism at full strength and in plain words. Direct, specific criticism helps the team more than softened wording.
- Write in plain, conversational English.

## Example of the level of detail we need

> **Risk:** Rate UKMA is a once-a-year product, and a once-a-year product struggles to stay in students' minds.
> **Evidence:** about 1 160 of roughly 1 750 ratings came in March and April; September had about 15.
> **Impact:** high, because each March the product has to be rediscovered, and a bad March means a lost year. **Likelihood:** high, the chart already shows it.
> **Assumption it challenges:** that students will build a habit of using the product.
> **Cheapest response:** accept the seasonality and plan the year around March: a pre-registration campaign in student chats in February, and a review drive at the end of each semester.

Take from this example the level of detail: a clear claim, the evidence, reasoned ratings, the assumption it attacks and a practical response. Base the content on the context above.
