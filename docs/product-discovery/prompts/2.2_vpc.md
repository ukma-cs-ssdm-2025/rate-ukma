# Prompt 2.2: Value Proposition Canvas

## Role

You are a product strategist who works with the Value Proposition Canvas and builds products for university students. You help the Rate UKMA team match what their users need against what the product actually delivers today.

## Goal

For each user profile, break the needs down into Jobs, Pains and Gains, and show exactly how Rate UKMA responds to them. The result goes into the README and becomes the input for the critical analysis in the next step. So tie every product decision to a specific need, and show the gaps (places where the product does not yet meet a need) as clearly as its strengths.

## Context

### How NaUKMA students choose courses

- Every year a student builds an individual study plan (ІНП) in САЗ, the university's course registration system (my.ukma.edu.ua). САЗ adds mandatory courses automatically. Profession-oriented electives come from the student's own specialty list. Free-choice courses can come from the plan of any specialty at the same level, including other faculties.
- A plan is about 60 credits a year, with a cap on weekly hours.
- Main registration runs in March for the whole next academic year. Students register for courses at the same time. So the decision has to be made before registration opens.
- A course with too few registrations does not take place, and its students are removed from it. A backup is needed.
- After correction week, the plan is locked: every course in it is mandatory, and skipping one leads to academic debt and can cost a scholarship.
- The timetable is published before each semester, after main registration.

### What a student sees in САЗ

Title, teacher, department, credits and hours, assessment type, annotation, group limits, number of enrolled students and free seats, the list of enrolled students, and course pages from past years. A catalog with search and filters. A credit count for the plan the student has already built.

### What САЗ lacks

The real workload, the quality and usefulness of a course, the teaching style of a specific teacher, an easy way to compare several courses, and a way to plan several variants of the study plan before registration. Students collect this information informally: seniors, friends, chats, and strangers found in last year's enrolment lists.

### What Rate UKMA offers

**Live now:**

- rating a course on difficulty and usefulness (1–5), with an optional written review;
- only students who were enrolled in a course can rate it (enrolment data comes from САЗ); login with a corporate @ukma.edu.ua account;
- reviews are published anonymously;
- a course map (usefulness × difficulty) and per-course analytics;
- the number of students enrolled in САЗ, shown on the course page;
- search and filters by specialty, teacher, semester, credits and rating;
- comments on reviews and helpful / unhelpful votes;
- a personal page with the student's own ratings, grouped by year;
- a feed of the latest reviews;
- notifications about votes and comments on the student's own reviews.

**In development this semester:** an ІНП planner: adding a course to the plan from its page, automatic credit and limit checks, several plan variants.

**In the backlog:** course recommendations based on similar course sets, reminders to rate completed courses, AI-generated tags from reviews for filtering.

**Open questions about the product:**

- whether free seats from САЗ are shown in Rate UKMA;
- whether ratings can be viewed separately for each teacher of the same course.

Treat these as unknown and mark any conclusion that depends on them.

### Behaviour we already observe

- Activity rises sharply in March, during main registration.
- About half of all ratings include a written review.

## User profiles (from the audience analysis, step 2.1)

### Key findings

- **March is a high-stakes, time-boxed decision with poor information.** The pressure comes from the registration queue, the risk of cancellation, plan lock-in and choosing without a timetable.
- **Students need course information at four moments:** one to three weeks before registration (the main one), registration week, correction week, and the end of each semester (when they have fresh experience to share, the supply side).
- **The least access to informal advice:** first-year master's students from other universities, students choosing free-choice courses in another faculty, students in small specialties, first-year bachelors facing their first registration.
- **The uncomfortable hypothesis:** the students who need reviews most are the least likely to find them. Cross-faculty and niche electives probably have the fewest reviews, and popular courses in large faculties the most.
- **Teacher changes:** ratings are tied to a course, and a course can change with its teacher, so aggregated ratings may mislead at the moment of choice.

### Profile 1. The pre-registration planner with no one to ask

- **Essence:** must pick many courses for the next year at once, with a main choice and a backup for each slot, before the queue opens, and knows almost no one who took these courses.
- **Who:** a second-year Political Science bachelor planning their third year. Much of the third-year plan is electives, so they have to pick many courses at once: profession-oriented courses from their own specialty and free-choice courses, some from other faculties. Has 15–20 candidate courses and a credit target; the annotations all sound alike, the teachers are unfamiliar, and their friends are in the same year and have not taken these courses either.
- **Trigger:** early-to-mid March, one to two weeks before registration opens.
- **Today:** opens САЗ pages one by one and keeps notes in their head or a spreadsheet; treats the enrolled count as a popularity signal; asks in the specialty chat and hears "didn't take it"; scans last year's enrolment lists for familiar names; sometimes messages strangers.
- **Success:** the night before registration, a ranked shortlist with a main pick for each slot, one or two backups for risky courses, credits that add up, and a clear idea of each course's real workload. A feeling of having chosen rather than guessed.
- **Barriers and fears:** being stuck for a semester with a heavy or useless course; the wanted course filling up while they decide; zero or two reviews on the exact courses they care about; old reviews that may describe a different teacher.
- **Frequency:** once a year, intensively for one to two weeks in March. Low frequency, high stakes, no habit.

### Profile 2. The correction-week scrambler

- **Essence:** a chosen course was cancelled, and there are a few days to find a replacement among courses that still have seats.
- **Who:** a third-year Computer Science bachelor, removed from a niche free-choice course that did not take place, three credits short, and sitting in the reserve for another course that may or may not take place.
- **Trigger:** the day first-stage statuses are published and correction week starts. Pressure measured in days.
- **Today:** filters САЗ for courses with free seats and the right credits, opens each one, asks in chats "does anyone know anything about X?", often takes whatever fits the credits and timetable.
- **Success:** within one or two evenings, a replacement that closes the credit gap, has seats and is not a disaster, plus an informed call on the reserve course: wait or switch.
- **Barriers and fears:** ending up with a leftover course nobody wanted for a good reason; missing the deadline; having to cross-check free seats in САЗ against ratings in Rate UKMA.
- **Frequency:** only in years when a choice gets cancelled; very focused for a few days.
- **Relation to Profile 1:** often the same student two to three weeks later. Kept separate because the task and the data needed differ: Profile 1 compares quality and workload across a long list with a week to think; Profile 2 needs "still open, fits my credits, not bad" within days.

### Profile 3. The contributor (senior student with fresh experience)

- **Essence:** has finished a course and can turn that experience into the ratings and reviews Profiles 1 and 2 depend on.
- **Who:** a fourth-year Sociology bachelor with 25+ courses behind them, including cross-faculty electives; exactly the senior people ask in chats; graduating in a year.
- **Trigger (both unconfirmed):** the end of a semester, especially after strong emotions about a course; or during their own March planning visit, when they see past courses on their personal page and rate a few. The March spike hints at the second.
- **Today:** mostly records nothing; answers chat questions one by one, and the answers get lost in chat history.
- **Success:** rating takes under a minute; they see their review helped (votes, views); they feel safe and sure the teacher cannot identify them.
- **Barriers and fears:** loss of anonymity in small groups, especially if they will meet the teacher again or need them for a thesis; no personal benefit; the effort of writing text; not knowing whether anyone reads their reviews.
- **Frequency:** unknown; potentially twice a year, realistically probably once, in March.
- **Relation to Profile 1:** a senior student plays both roles. Kept separate because this is the supply side and the motivation is different: altruism or emotion instead of personal stake.

### Anti-persona and stakeholders

- **Anti-persona, the score-settler:** comes to punish a teacher after a conflict or a bad grade, writes emotional one-star reviews about the person rather than the course. Served with moderation and voting.
- **Deprioritised:** students whose plans are nearly all mandatory, such as first-year bachelors in their first semester.
- **Stakeholders:** teachers (public ratings affect registrations and therefore whether their course runs), departments (ratings can shift students between them), the university administration and the САЗ team (control login and the enrolment data that make reviews verified; the most critical stakeholder), student government (a possible ally for promotion and recruiting contributors).

## Task

For each of the three profiles:

1. **Jobs.** List what the person is trying to get done, in three types:
   - functional: what has to be done (for example, build a plan for the year);
   - emotional: how they want to feel (for example, confident in the choice);
   - social: how they want to be seen or what they want to be for others (for example, helpful to younger students).
   Mark the 2–3 most important jobs.
2. **Pains.** List obstacles, risks and bad outcomes linked to those jobs. For each pain, give its severity (high / medium / low) and the job it belongs to.
3. **Gains.** List the outcomes the person wants: required (the solution fails without them), expected, desired and unexpected. Give each one an importance level.
4. **Products & Services.** List the Rate UKMA features relevant to this profile, each with a status: live / in development / backlog.
5. **Pain Relievers.** For each pain, name the feature that relieves it and explain how. When no feature covers a pain yet, mark it as a gap.
6. **Gain Creators.** For each gain, name the feature that creates it and explain how. Mark gaps the same way.
7. **Fit.** Assess how well the product covers the three most severe pains and the three most important gains of this profile. Name the gaps that hurt usefulness the most.
8. **Value proposition.** Write one sentence using this template:
   "For [profile] who [job], Rate UKMA [what it gives], so that [main gain], unlike [current alternative]."

After all three profiles:

9. **Links between profiles.** Show how the gains of one profile depend on the actions of another. In particular, show how Profiles 1 and 2 depend on Profile 3, and test the uncomfortable hypothesis from the key findings against the value map. Say which profile the product serves best today and why. Note which features also protect the product from the anti-persona.

## How to work

- Write Jobs, Pains and Gains in the student's own words and situation. Keep product features in the Value Map only. The left side of the canvas describes the person; the right side describes the product.
- Build on the registration mechanics and the key findings above: the queue, the cancellation risk, plan lock-in and the timetable arriving after registration create the most severe pains.
- Tie every Pain Reliever and Gain Creator to one specific feature and one specific pain or gain.
- Give every feature a status, so it is clear what the product delivers today and what it promises.
- State the condition under which a feature works (for example, "works when the course has at least a few reviews").
- When a conclusion rests on an assumption or on an open product question, mark it as a hypothesis.
- Write in plain, conversational English with concrete words.

## Example of the level of detail we need

> **Pain:** "I'm taking a course from another faculty and I don't know anyone who took it. The annotation sounds interesting, but I have no idea how much time it will eat." Severity: high. Job: choose free-choice courses.
>
> **Pain Reliever:** difficulty rating and written reviews on the course page (live). The student sees the real workload from people who took the course, without hunting for acquaintances in another faculty. Works when the course has at least a few reviews.

Take from this example the level of detail: the student's situation, severity, the link to a job, a specific feature, its status and the condition under which it works. Base the content on the profiles and context above.
