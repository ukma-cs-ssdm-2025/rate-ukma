# Rate UKMA: Target Audience Analysis

## Analysis

**What creates the most pressure.** The single biggest pressure point is the simultaneous queue. Popular courses fill up in seconds, so the real decision has to be made before registration opens. Nobody has time to research once the clock starts. Three other factors add to this:

- **Cancellation risk.** Any course can end up "not taking place," so a student needs a backup for every risky choice, not just a first pick.
- **Plan fixation.** After correction week, everything in the plan is mandatory, and skipping a course means academic debt. A bad choice in March costs a full semester of consequences.
- **Blind scheduling.** The timetable comes out after the main registration, so in March students commit without knowing if their courses clash.

Together these make March a high-stakes, time-boxed decision with poor information, which explains the March activity spike.

**When students need course information.** There are four moments:

1. **One to three weeks before registration opens.** Building a shortlist plus backups. This is the main moment.
2. **Registration week.** Quick swaps when a course is full or the plan doesn't add up.
3. **Correction week.** Replacing courses that didn't take place, choosing only from courses with free seats, under days of time pressure.
4. **End of each semester** (December–January, May–June). Not a moment of need, but the moment when a student has fresh experience to share. This is the supply side of the product.

There may also be a fifth moment before each semester, when choosing a specific group, if different groups of the same course have different teachers. The context doesn't say whether that happens, so this is a data gap.

**Who has the least access to informal advice:**

- **First-year master's students, especially from other universities.** They arrive in September and face March registration six months later, knowing almost no seniors.
- **Anyone picking free-choice courses from another faculty.** Their specialty chat has no one who took the course, and the list of last year's students is full of strangers.
- **Students in small specialties.** A small chat means few people have taken any given elective.
- **First-year bachelors facing their first March registration.** They have friends, but mostly in the same year, which means equally inexperienced.

**The uncomfortable part.** The people who most need reviews are the least likely to find them. Reviews come from students who took a course, and most students probably take courses within their own faculty. So cross-faculty and niche electives, where newcomers need help most, likely have the fewest reviews. Popular courses in big faculties, where informal advice is already easy to get, likely have the most. If this holds, Rate UKMA currently gives the most value to the students who need it least. This is a hypothesis, and it's cheap to check (see the table).

A second risk: ratings are tied to a course, but a course can change along with its teacher. Aggregated ratings across different teachers may mislead exactly at the moment of choice.

## Result

### Profile 1. The pre-registration planner with no one to ask

**Essence:** A student who must pick many courses for the next year at once, with a main choice and a backup for each slot, before the queue opens, and knows almost no one who took these courses.

**Who:** A second-year Political Science bachelor planning their third year. Much of the third-year plan is electives, so they have to pick many courses at once: profession-oriented courses from their own specialty and free-choice courses, some of them from the Faculty of Informatics. They have a list of 15–20 candidate courses from the САЗ catalog and a credit target to hit. The annotations all sound similar, and they recognize none of the teachers. Their friends are in the same year and haven't taken these courses either.

**Trigger:** Early-to-mid March, one to two weeks before registration opens. The registration date is announced, and the student realizes they can't decide by reading annotations alone.

**How they solve it now:** They open САЗ pages one by one and keep notes in their head or a spreadsheet. They treat the enrolled count as a popularity signal. They ask in their specialty chat and usually get "didn't take it, don't know." They scan last year's enrolled list for a familiar name and often find none. Sometimes they message strangers from that list.

**Success:** The night before registration, they have a ranked shortlist: a main pick for each slot, one or two backups for courses likely to be cancelled, and credits that add up to the target. They understand the real workload of each course and feel they chose rather than guessed.

**Barriers and fears:**

- Fear of getting stuck for a whole semester with a heavy or useless course they can't leave.
- Fear that the course they want fills up while they're still deciding.
- The courses they care about may have zero or two reviews, which makes the product useless for this particular case.
- They can't tell if old reviews describe the current teacher.

**Frequency:** Once a year, intensively for one to two weeks in March. For a bachelor, the choice before the third year is likely one of the biggest of their studies, because so much of the plan is open. This is a low-frequency, high-stakes user, and they won't form a habit.

### Profile 2. The correction-week scrambler

**Essence:** A student whose chosen course was cancelled, and who has a few days to find a replacement among courses that still have seats.

**Who:** A third-year Computer Science bachelor. They registered for a niche free-choice course that got the "did not take place" status and were automatically removed. They're now three credits short. Another course is "may take place," and they're in the reserve, unsure whether to wait or give up the spot.

**Trigger:** The day the first-stage statuses are published and correction week starts. The pressure is acute, measured in days rather than weeks.

**How they solve it now:** They filter САЗ for courses with free seats and the right credits, then open each one. They ask in chats, "Does anyone know anything about X?" They often take whatever fits the credits and schedule gaps, not what they'd actually want. Under time pressure, the enrolled count and free seats matter more than quality.

**Success:** Within one or two evenings, they find a replacement that fills the credit gap, has seats, and isn't a disaster. They also make an informed call on the reserve course: wait or switch.

**Barriers and fears:**

- Fear of ending up with a random leftover course that nobody wanted for good reason.
- Fear of missing the deadline and being short on credits.
- In Rate UKMA, they can't see which courses still have seats unless that data comes through from САЗ. If they have to cross-check between two sites, the product loses to САЗ alone.

**Frequency:** Only in years when one of their choices gets cancelled. When it happens, they're very focused for a few days.

**Same person as Profile 1?** Yes, often it's the same student two to three weeks later. We still treat them separately, because the task and the data needed are different. Profile 1 compares quality and workload across a long list with a week to think. Profile 2 needs "what's still open, fits my credits, and isn't bad," within days. This leads to different features. For Profile 1, it's comparison and a planner with backup variants. For Profile 2, it's a "still available" filter joined with ratings and a quick credit gap check. If the planner with multiple variants works well in March, part of Profile 2's pain disappears, since the backup is already prepared. That's worth measuring.

### Profile 3. The contributor (senior student with fresh experience)

**Essence:** A student who has finished a course and can turn their experience into ratings and reviews that Profiles 1 and 2 depend on.

**Who:** A fourth-year Sociology bachelor. Over three years, they've taken 25+ courses, including several cross-faculty electives. They are exactly the senior people in chats ask for advice, and they'll graduate in a year, taking that knowledge with them.

**Trigger (two scenarios, both unconfirmed):**

- **End of semester.** After a final exam, especially with strong emotions about the course, whether very positive or very negative.
- **During their own March planning visit.** They come for Profile 1's task, see their past courses on their personal page, and rate a few while they're there.

The March activity spike suggests the second scenario might be the main one, but there's no data yet on when ratings are actually written.

**How they solve it now:** Mostly, they don't record anything. They answer questions in chats when asked, one by one, and the answer disappears in the chat history.

**Success:** Rating a course takes under a minute. They see that their review was useful (upvotes, views). They feel it's safe, and the teacher won't figure out who wrote it.

**Barriers and fears:**

- **Loss of anonymity in small groups.** In a course of eight students, an "anonymous" review about a specific situation is easy to trace back. This is especially risky if they'll have the same teacher again or need them for a thesis.
- **No personal benefit.** They've already made their choices.
- **Time.** Writing a meaningful text takes effort, which may explain why only about half of ratings include text.
- **Wasted effort.** They don't know if anyone reads their reviews.

**Frequency:** Unknown. That's one of the open questions. Potentially twice a year after each semester, realistically probably once a year in March.

**Same person as Profile 1?** Yes, a senior student plays both roles. They need to be considered separately because this is the supply side. Without contributors, Profiles 1 and 2 get an empty page. The motivation is also fundamentally different: Profile 1 has a personal stake, while Profile 3 has none and acts out of altruism or emotion. The most important product decision for Profile 3 is when to ask for a rating, and the March visit may be the best moment.

### Anti-persona and stakeholders

**Anti-persona: the score-settler.** A student who comes to punish a specific teacher after a conflict or a bad grade. They write emotional one-star reviews about the person rather than the course, and possibly several ratings in a row. We serve them last. Their content helps nobody choose, it damages trust in the ratings, and it increases the risk of conflict with teachers and the administration, which could threaten the product itself (see below). For them, we build moderation and voting, not features.

A second group we deprioritize without excluding: **students with nearly all-mandatory plans.** For example, first-year bachelors in their first semester, where САЗ adds courses automatically. The product can't help them yet, and there's no reason to design for them.

**Stakeholders (affected, but no access):**

- **Teachers.** Their reputation is publicly rated, and they can't see or respond. A low rating means fewer registrations, which can lead to "did not take place," which affects the teacher's workload. This is a real consequence, not just an image issue.
- **Departments.** Course enrollment determines whether their offerings run. Ratings can redistribute students between departments, especially for free-choice courses.
- **University administration and the САЗ team.** This is the most critical stakeholder. Login via @ukma.edu.ua and data about courses and enrollments come from САЗ. If the administration restricts that access due to teacher complaints or data protection concerns, the product loses its main advantage: verified reviews.
- **Student government.** A potential ally for promotion and legitimacy, and possibly a channel for recruiting contributors.
